# 02 — Colors and archetypes

[Research index](README.md) · Next: [Skeleton and distribution](03-skeleton-and-distribution.md)

## What is an archetype?

An archetype is a repeatable deck plan supported by the environment. A color pair describes access to cards; an archetype explains why a particular subset belongs together. A faction gives that grouping a fictional identity. A mechanic supplies a rules tool. These can align, but are not interchangeable.

Rosewater describes ten two-color archetypes and a cycle of two-color uncommon signposts as a common default. He derives archetypes first from the set's major mechanics and themes, then their supporting resources, then themes needed to complete the structure. A large theme can serve several archetypes, differentiated by speed and execution. [Limited themes](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/nuts-bolts-12-part-2-limited-themes-2020-03-16).

**Synthesis:** a usable archetype brief answers six questions: What starts it? What resource matters? What rewards that resource? How does it win? What ordinary cards keep it functional? Why choose it over another deck sharing a color?

## Strategy families versus synergy themes

This working vocabulary distinguishes how a deck plays from what its cards care about:

| Strategy family | Practical plan |
|---|---|
| Aggro | Establish pressure early and end the game before slower investments pay off. |
| Tempo | Combine a clock with temporary disruption, making time more important than lasting card advantage. |
| Midrange | Trade resources and deploy efficient threats, changing roles with the matchup. |
| Control | Survive early pressure, answer threats, then win through superior later resources. |
| Engine or combo | Assemble interacting pieces that produce disproportionate value or a decisive result. |

These are analytical categories, not exclusive boxes. “Artifacts,” “graveyard,” “tokens,” and “spells” describe synergy themes that can support several families. “Typal” means caring about creature types; it does not specify a speed. An engine can support a midrange deck without requiring an all-or-nothing combo. The case studies show why both axes belong in an archetype brief.

## Color philosophy and mechanical identity

Wizards' philosophical model associates white with peace and order, blue with improvement and knowledge, black with power and self-interest, red with freedom and emotional action, and green with acceptance and natural interdependence. These are motivations that produce conflicts, not a good/evil classification. [Pie Fights](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/pie-fights-2016-11-14).

Its mechanical color pie assigns effects at primary, secondary, and tertiary levels, indicating frequency and access with conditions. The 2021 reference is explicitly a dated snapshot. [Mechanical Color Pie 2021](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/mechanical-color-pie-2021).

This abbreviated vocabulary is a research orientation, not a card-legality validator:

| Color | Common mechanical resources | Typical design constraint |
|---|---|---|
| White | Small creatures, coordination, protection, conditional removal | Its answers and card advantage often depend on circumstances. |
| Blue | Card selection, countermagic, bounce, flying | Returning a threat is different from permanently destroying it. |
| Black | Creature destruction, sacrifice, graveyard access, resource payment | Powerful exchanges often consume life, cards, or creatures. |
| Red | Damage, haste, temporary resources, impulsive play | Damage has size limits; immediate access need not mean lasting advantage. |
| Green | Large creatures, mana development, creature-based interaction | Its interaction often requires creatures rather than bypassing them. |

For new cards, consult the relevant effect and later changes rather than extrapolating from this shorthand.

## The ten pairs

W = white, U = blue, B = black, R = red, G = green. “Allied” and “enemy” refer to positions in the color wheel. Enemy pairs are not inherently weaker, less supported, or forbidden from cooperation.

The following is a **synthesized idea menu**, not Wizards' fixed assignment for every set. Its examples combine the mechanical vocabulary above with the published Dominaria, Strixhaven, Bloomburrow, and Duskmourn archetypes discussed in [chapter 6](06-case-studies.md).

| Pair | Relationship | Ravnica shorthand | Familiar directions worth exploring | A distinction to specify |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WU | Allied | Azorius | Flying pressure; defensive control; repeatable enter-the-battlefield value | Is evasion racing or ending a long game? |
| UB | Allied | Dimir | Control; graveyard setup; evasive incremental advantage | Is the graveyard fuel, a threshold, or a source of threats? |
| BR | Allied | Rakdos | Sacrifice; aggressive life loss; expendable resources | Does it need fodder or simply successful attacks? |
| RG | Allied | Gruul | Large-creature pressure; ramp; high-power rewards | Does it accelerate or curve out naturally? |
| GW | Allied | Selesnya | Token armies; counters; enchantment-supported creatures | Is it building many bodies or improving a few? |
| WB | Enemy | Orzhov | Life-total engines; sacrifice; recursion and reanimation | Does life buy time, trigger rewards, or pay costs? |
| UR | Enemy | Izzet | Cheap-spell tempo; expensive-spell value; noncreature engines | Many spells, large spells, or a particular permanent type? |
| BG | Enemy | Golgari | Graveyard value; attrition; sacrifice; creature-based engines | Does it preserve the graveyard or spend it? |
| RW | Enemy | Boros | Aggressive teamwork; targeted creature buffs; equipment; occasional slower recursion | What changes its decisions beyond “attack”? |
| GU | Enemy | Simic | Ramp; large creatures; repeated creature entry; resource growth | What turns accumulated resources into victory? |

Guild names are convenient nicknames, not obligations to reproduce Ravnica's organizations. Strixhaven deliberately explored different identities for existing pairs; its red-white college used history and the graveyard rather than defaulting to an aggressive military faction. [In the Strixhaven, Part 2](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/strixhaven-part-2-2021-04-05).

## Signposts and support

The Dominaria guide uses legendary gold uncommons to introduce its ten strategies: their text communicates which surrounding cards a drafter should seek. A signpost can therefore teach both the pair's colors and its gameplay incentive. [Dominaria archetypes](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/whirlwind-tour-archetypes-dominaria-2018-04-24).

**Synthesis:** a signpost makes a promise. Commons and other accessible cards must fulfill it. A rare that rewards an unsupported condition is a lottery ticket, not a dependable archetype. A useful signpost need not be the strongest standalone card; it should make its deck plan legible and worth considering.

For an invented sacrifice archetype, distinguish:

- **Enabler:** supplies disposable creatures or another useful resource.
- **Outlet:** lets the player spend or sacrifice that resource.
- **Payoff:** rewards the sacrifice or resulting death.
- **Bridge:** has a useful role in another deck too.
- **Baseline playable:** contributes to combat or interaction without assembling the engine.

A card can fill more than one role. Count actual functionality, not just tags: a sacrifice outlet that requires another scarce resource might not reliably enable anything on its own.

## Overlap and draft decisions

Wizards' synergy discussion emphasizes that simple cards can produce deeper interactions in combination. Players discover relationships without each card needing a large amount of explanatory text. [Living in Synergy](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/living-synergy-2013-02-25).

Consider an **invented** cheap creature that leaves a token when it dies. It could be desirable to a sacrifice deck, a token deck, and a defensive deck. Those drafters compete for it for different reasons. That produces more interesting choices than labeling it “BR-only support.” The opposite extreme also matters: if every deck wants every card equally, there is little reason to discover a specialized plan.

A useful review exercise is to choose each monocolor card and ask which neighboring strategies want it, which want it most, and which should pass it. Require an explanation grounded in play. “It is in their colors” is insufficient.

## Gold, hybrid, fixing, and commitment

Gold asks the deck to supply multiple colors. Hybrid permits alternate colors of payment. Rosewater's hybrid-design discussion argues that hybrid should function acceptably when paid entirely with either color, while documenting internal disagreement over some historical implementations. Use that as a conservative design principle, not a claim that every printed hybrid card follows one uncontested rule. [Mix & Match, Part I](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/mix-match-part-i-2008-04-28).

**Synthesis:** gold cards can reward commitment; hybrid cards can widen access. Fixing determines whether that commitment is meaningful. Do not treat colorless cards as a sixth faction or “multicolored” as one additional color with the same quota as white. Their draft audiences differ dramatically.

## Alternatives to ten equal lanes

Three-color faction sets need routes into their factions. Wizards explains that an enemy pair can lead into two wedges: WB can become Abzan by adding green or Mardu by adding red. That gives a drafter room to discover the third color. [A Dragonstorm Is Brewing, Part 1](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/a-dragonstorm-is-brewing-part-1).

For any proposed structure, explicitly identify the primary decks, secondary decks, and transitions between them. Ten independent factions are not automatically better than five, and ten available pairs do not imply ten players must each occupy a distinct lane.
